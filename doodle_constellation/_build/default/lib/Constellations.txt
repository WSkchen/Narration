Andromeda.Cassiopeia, the queen of Aethiopia, bragged that her daughter was more beautiful than the Nereids, sea nymphs blessed with incredible beauty.\nOffended at her remark, the nymphs petitioned Poseidon to punish Cassiopeia for her insolence, which he did by commanding the sea monster Cetus to attack Aethiopia.\nAndromeda's panicked father, Cepheus, was told by the Oracle of Ammon that the only way to save his kingdom was to sacrifice his daughter to Cetus.\nShe was chained to a rock by the sea but was saved by the hero Perseus
Antlia.Originally Antlia Pneumatica, the constellation was established by Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille in the 18th century.\nIts non-specific (single-word) name, already in limited use, was preferred by John Herschel then welcomed by the astronomic community which officially accepted this.\nNorth of stars forming some of the sails of the ship Argo Navis (the constellation Vela).
Apus.It represents a bird-of-paradise, and its name means "without feet" in Greek because the bird-of-paradise was once wrongly believed to lack feet. First depicted on a celestial globe by Petrus Plancius in 1598, it was charted on a star atlas by Johann Bayer in his 1603 Uranometria.
Aquarius.One of the Zodiacs. Its name is Latin for "water-carrier" or "cup-carrier".\nAssociated with various divinities, but always with water and floods.
Aquila.Its name is Latin for 'eagle'.\nSome sources claim it represents the bird that carried Zeus/Jupiter's thunderbolts in Greco-Roman mythology, while others say it was the bird that Zeus ordered to attack Prometheus.
Ara.Ara was identified as the altar where the gods first made offerings and formed an alliance before defeating the Titans
Aries.One of the Zodiacs. This constellation is associated with the golden ram of Greek mythology that rescued Phrixus and Helle on orders from Hermes, taking Phrixus to the land of Colchis.
Auriga.Auriga is often identified as the hero Erichthonius of Athens, the chthonic son of Hephaestus who was raised by the goddess Athena.\nErichthonius was generally credited to be the inventor of the quadriga, the four-horse chariot, which he used in the battle against the usurper Amphictyon, the event that made Erichthonius the king of Athens.
Boötes.Was mentioned in the Odyssey as a celestial reference for maritime navigation, describing it as "late-setting" or "slow to set". Various greek myths(and also other myths I'm too lazy too include) could be represented here
Caelum.Formerly known as Caelum Sculptorium ("Engraver's Chisel").
Camelopardalis.It's a camel. Nice.
Cancer.One of the Zodiacs.Latin for "crab", aka the "Dark Sign" due to its dimness.\nIn Greek mythology, Cancer is identified with the crab that appeared while Heracles fought the many-headed Lernaean Hydra.\nHeracles slew the crab after it bit him in the foot.\nAfterwards, the goddess Hera, an enemy of Heracles, placed the crab among the stars
Canes Venatici.Its name is Latin for 'hunting dogs', and the constellation is often depicted in illustrations as representing the dogs of Boötes the Herdsman, a neighboring constellation.
Canis Major."Greater Dog", contains Sirius aka the "Dog Star", represents several dogs from Greek Mythology
Canis Minor."Lesser Dog", also related to several European mythical dogs, in China it is a bird(the Vermilion Bird of the South).
Capricornus."Horned Goat",  sometimes identified as Amalthea, the goat that suckled the infant Zeus after his mother, Rhea, saved him from being devoured by his father, Cronos.\nAmalthea's broken horn was transformed into the cornucopia or "horn of plenty".\nCould also represent Pan, Greek god of nature/wilderness.
Carina.Its name is Latin for the keel of a ship, and it was the southern foundation of the larger constellation of Argo Navis (the ship Argo which was used by Jason and the Argonauts searching for the Golden Fleece).
Cassiopeia.Cassiopeia was the wife of King Cepheus of Aethiopia and mother of Princess Andromeda.\nCepheus and Cassiopeia were placed next to each other among the stars, along with Andromeda.\nShe was placed in the sky as a punishment after enraging Poseidon with the boast that her daughter Andromeda was more beautiful than the Nereids or, alternatively, that she herself was more beautiful than the sea nymphs.\nShe was forced to wheel around the north celestial pole on her throne, spending half of her time clinging to it so she does not fall off.
Centaurus.Centaurus represents a centaur; a creature that is half human, half horse.\nIncludes the star Alpha Centauri!
Cepheus.Cepheus was the King of Aethiopia. He was married to Cassiopeia and was the father of Andromeda
Cetus.It is often now called the Whale, though it is most strongly associated with Cetus the sea-monster, who was slain by Perseus as he saved the princess Andromeda from Poseidon's wrath
Chamaeleon.Congratulations! You found a Chamaeleon among the stars.
Circinus.Its name is Latin for compass, referring to the drafting tool used for drawing circles.
Columba.Its name is Latin for dove.
Coma Berenices.Its name means "Berenice's Hair" in Latin and refers to Queen Berenice II of Egypt, who sacrificed her long hair as a religious offering.
Corona Australis.Its Latin name means "southern crown", and it is the southern counterpart of Corona Borealis, the northern crown.\nFrequently associated with various mythical crowns.
Corona Borealis.Its Latin name, inspired by its shape, means "northern crown".\nIn classical mythology Corona Borealis generally represented the crown given by the god Dionysus to the Cretan princess Ariadne and set by her in the heavens.
Corvus.It depicts a raven, a bird associated with stories about the god Apollo, perched on the back of Hydra the water snake.\nThe crow turned constellation is either punished for telling Apollo about adultery or for lying to him.
Crater.Its name is the Latinization of the Greek krater, a type of cup used to water down wine.\nOne version where Corvus lies, and is cast into the stars alongside a cup it can never reach.
Crux.Centred on four bright stars in a cross-shaped asterism commonly known as the Southern Cross.\nUsed to be part of Centaurus, until Earth rotated it away.
Cygnus.Latinized Greek word for "swan". Contains the Northern Cross.\nIn Greek mythology, Cygnus has been identified with several different legendary swans. Zeus disguised himself as a swan to seduce Leda, Spartan king Tyndareus's wife, who gave birth to the Gemini, Helen of Troy, and Clytemnestra, and Orpheus was transformed into a swan after his murder and said to have been placed in the sky next to his lyre (Lyra).
Delphinus.Its name is the Latin version for the Greek word for dolphin.\nIs associated with either being Poseidon's wingman(fin?) with Amphitrite or a separate dolphin
Dorado. Its name refers to the mahi-mahi (Coryphaena hippurus), which is known as golden in Spanish, although it has also been depicted as a swordfish.
Draco.Its name is derived from the Latin word for dragon.\nDraco was identified with several different dragons in Greek mythology.
Equuleus.Its name is Latin for "little horse".\nIn Greek mythology, one myth associates Equuleus with the foal Celeris, who was the offspring or brother of the winged horse Pegasus. Celeris was given to Castor by Mercury.\nOther myths say that Equuleus is the horse struck from Poseidon's trident, during the contest between him and Athena when deciding which would be the superior.
Eridanus.It is represented as a river.\nEridanus is connected to the myth of Phaethon, who took over the reins of his father Helios' sky chariot, but did not have the strength to control it and so veered wildly in different directions, scorching both Earth and heaven. Zeus intervened by striking Phaethon dead with a thunderbolt and casting him to Earth.\nThe constellation was supposed to be the path Phaethon drove along.
Fornax.Its name is Latin for furnace.\nThe Hubble Space Telescope's Ultra-Deep Field is located within Fornax
Gemini.One of the Zodiacs. Its name is Latin for twins, and it is associated with the twins Castor and Pollux in Greek mythology.
Grus.Its name is Latin for the crane, a type of bird.\nUsed to be part of the Pisces constellation.
Hercules.Hercules is a constellation named after Hercules, the Roman mythological hero adapted from the Greek hero Heracles.\nThe full myth is too long to fit, but Hera hates Hercules and screws him over(repeatedly).
Horologium.The pendulum clock. One of 14 IAU designated Constellations found by a certain French dude.
Hydra.Hydra is the largest of the 88 modern constellations.\nOne myth associates it with a water snake that a crow served Apollo in a cup when it was sent to fetch water.\nApollo saw through the fraud and angrily cast the crow, cup and snake into the sky.\nIt is also associated with the monster Hydra, with its many heads, killed by Hercules.
Hydrus.Its name means "male water snake", as opposed to Hydra, a much larger constellation that represents a female water snake.
Indus.The English translation of its name is given as the Indian, apparently representing an inhabitant of Madgascar or the East Indies.
Lacerta.Its name is Latin for lizard.\nts brightest stars form a "W" shape similar to that of Cassiopeia, and it is thus sometimes referred to as 'Little Cassiopeia'.
Leo.One of the Zodiacs. Its name is Latin for lion, and to the ancient Greeks represented the Nemean Lion killed by the mythical Greek hero Heracles as one of his twelve labors.
Leo Minor.Its name is Latin for "the smaller lion", in contrast to Leo, the larger lion.
Lepus.Its name is Latin for hare. It is located below—immediately south—of Orion (the hunter), and is sometimes represented as a hare being chased by Orion or by Orion's hunting dogs.
Libra.One of the Zodiacs. Its name is Latin for weighing scales. Was associated with scorpion claws in some mythologies.
Lupus.Its name is Latin for wolf. Was part of Centaurus, specifically in the act of dying for Centaurus.
Lynx.Named after the actual animal.
Lyra.Latin for Lyre(the musical instrument), often represented on star maps as a vulture or an eagle carrying a lyre.\nIn Greek mythology, Lyra represents the lyre of Orpheus who was the greatest musician alive.
Mensa.Spanish for Table.\nOriginally named "Montagne de la Table" or "Mons Mensae", Mensa was created by Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille out of dim Southern Hemisphere stars in honor of Table Mountain, a South African mountain overlooking Cape Town, near the location of Lacaille's observatory.
Microscopium.The name is a Latinized form of the Greek word for microscope.
Monoceros.Greek for Unicorn. Relatively modern, so no attached myths.
Musca.Latin for Fly, aka Apis(Latin for Bee).
Norma.Its name is Latin for normal, referring to a right angle, and is variously considered to represent a rule, a carpenter's square, a set square or a level.
Octans.Its name is Latin for the eighth part of a circle, but it is named after the octant, a navigational instrument.
Ophiuchus.Its name comes from the Ancient Greek ὀφιοῦχος (ophioûkhos), meaning "serpent-bearer".\nTo the ancient Greeks, the constellation represented the god Apollo struggling with a huge snake that guarded the Oracle of Delphi.\nLater myths identified Ophiuchus with Laocoön, the Trojan priest of Poseidon, who warned his fellow Trojans about the Trojan Horse and was later slain by a pair of sea serpents sent by the gods to punish him.
Orion.In Greek mythology, Orion was a gigantic, supernaturally strong hunter, born to Euryale, a Gorgon, and Poseidon, god of the sea.\nOne myth recounts Gaia's rage at Orion, who dared to say that he would kill every animal on Earth. The angry goddess tried to dispatch Orion with a scorpion.\nThis is given as the reason that the constellations of Scorpius and Orion are never in the sky at the same time.\nHowever, Ophiuchus, the Serpent Bearer, revived Orion with an antidote. This is said to be the reason that the constellation of Ophiuchus stands midway between the Scorpion and the Hunter in the sky.
Pavo.Latin for 'peacock'.\nHera adorned the tail of a peacock—her favorite bird—with Argus Panoptes' eyes in his honor.
Pegasus.Named after the winged horse Pegasus in Greek mythology./nPegasus was born when Perseus cut off the head of Medusa, who was impregnated by the god Poseidon. He was born with Chrysaor from Medusa's blood.\nEventually, it became the horse for Bellerophon, who was asked to kill the Chimera and succeeded with the help of Athena and Pegasus.
Perseus.Named after the Greek mythological hero Perseus. Perseus was the son of Danaë, who was sent by King Polydectes to bring the head of Medusa the Gorgon—whose visage caused all who gazed upon her to turn to stone.\nPerseus slew Medusa in her sleep, and Pegasus and Chrysaor appeared from her body. On the way back, he rescued Andromeda, then killed his father with Medusa's head, and gave his step-father the crown.
Phoenix.Named after the mythical phoenix, a Greek originating sun related bird that dies and revives.
Pictor.Its name is Latin for painter, and is an abbreviation of the older name Equuleus Pictoris (the "painter's easel")
Pisces.One of the Zodiacs. Its name is Latin for "fishes".\nPisces is associated with the Greek legend that Aphrodite and her son Eros either shape-shifted into forms of fishes to escape, or were rescued by two fishes.
Piscis Austrinus.The name is Latin for "the southern fish", in contrast with the larger constellation Pisces, which represents a pair of fish.\nIn Greek mythology, this constellation is known as the Great Fish and it is portrayed as swallowing the water being poured out by Aquarius, the water-bearer constellation. The two fish of the constellation Pisces are said to be the offspring of the Great Fish.
Puppis.Latin for Stern, was originally part of the traditional constellation of Argo Navis (Jason and the Argonauts).
Pyxis.Abbreviated from Pyxis Nautica, its name is Latin for a mariner's compass.
Reticulum.Its name is Latin for a small net, or reticle—a net of crosshairs at the focus of a telescope eyepiece that is used to measure star positions.
Sagitta.Its name is Latin for 'arrow'.\nIt was regarded as the weapon that Hercules used to kill the eagle (Aquila) of Jove that perpetually gnawed Prometheus' liver.
Sagittarius.One of the Zodiacs. Its name is Latin for "archer".\nIn Greek mythology, Sagittarius is usually identified as a centaur: half human, half horse. However, perhaps due to the Greeks' adoption of the Sumerian constellation, some confusion surrounds the identity of the archer.
Scorpius.One of the Zodiacs. Scorpion in Latin or Greek.\nAfter Orion dies to the scorpion sent by Gaia/Artemis/Leto/Apollo, Artemis asks Zeus to turn him into a constellation.
Sculptor.It represents a sculptor. Originally named Apparatus Sculptoris (the sculptor's studio).
Scutum.Its name is Latin for shield, and it was originally named Scutum Sobiescianum
Serpens.Greek for Serpent. It is unique among the modern constellations in being split into two non-contiguous parts, Serpens Caput (Serpent Head) to the west and Serpens Cauda (Serpent Tail) to the east.\nBetween these two halves lies the constellation of Ophiuchus, the "Serpent-Bearer"
Sextans.Its name is Latin for the astronomical sextant, a toll used for measuring stars.
Taurus.One of the Zodiacs. Latin for Bull.\nIn Greek mythology, Taurus was identified with Zeus, who turned into a white bull to kidnap Europa and turned a lover into a bull to hide her. Taurus is also associated with one of Hercules' Twelve Labors.
Telescopium.Its name is a Latinized form of the Greek word for telescope.
Triangulum.Its name is Latin for "triangle", derived from its three brightest stars, which form a long and narrow triangle.\nThe Ancient Greeks called Triangulum Deltoton (Δελτωτόν), as the constellation resembled an upper-case Greek letter delta
Triangulum Australe. Its name is Latin for "the southern triangle", which distinguishes it from Triangulum in the northern sky and is derived from the acute, almost equilateral pattern of its three brightest stars.
Tucana.Named after the toucan, a South American bird.
Ursa Major.Its Latin name means "greater (or larger) bear", referring to and contrasting it with nearby Ursa Minor, the lesser bear.\nContains Polaris and the "Big Dipper".\nIn Greek mythology, Zeus lusts after a young woman named Callisto, a nymph of Artemis. Zeus's jealous wife Hera discovers that Callisto has a son named Arcas as the result of her rape by Zeus and transforms Callisto into a bear as a punishment.\nCallisto, while in bear form, later encounters her son Arcas. Arcas almost spears the bear, but to avert the tragedy Zeus whisks them both into the sky, Callisto as Ursa Major and Arcas as the constellation Boötes.
Ursa Minor.Latin for Lesser Bear, aka Little Dipper, referring to Ursa Major. Ursa Major appears to have stolen all the mythology.
Vela.Its name is Latin for the sails of a ship, and it was originally part of a larger constellation, the ship Argo Navis(Jason and the Argonauts)
Virgo.One of the Zodiacs. The name means "maiden" in Latin.\nThe Greeks and Romans identified the constellation with the goddesses Demeter and Persephone, as well as Astraea, a goddess of justice, who left earth during the end of the Silver Age and fled to the sky, becoming the constellation.\nA few other maidens are also associated.
Volans.It represents a flying fish; its name is a shortened form of its original name, Piscis Volans
Vulpecula.Its name is Latin for "little fox", although it is commonly called simply the Fox.
